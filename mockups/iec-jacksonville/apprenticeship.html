Apprenticeship

Get paid to learn the trade. Four years, one night a week.

An IEC apprentice works full time for an electrical contractor and goes to class one evening a week. There is no student debt at the end of it, because the employer pays the tuition.

Program overview

Earn while you learn — the way the trade has always done it.

Apprenticeship is not school with a job attached. It is a job with school attached. You are hired by an electrical contractor, you work their jobs Monday through Friday for a paycheck, and one evening a week you are in a classroom with an instructor who is a licensed electrician.

Over four years you accumulate the two things a state licensing board cares about: documented on-the-job hours — [8,000 hrs target] — and related classroom instruction on a registered curriculum. Get both and you sit for the exam.

IEC Jacksonville teaches the IEC national curriculum, which is registered with the U.S. Department of Labor and used by chapters nationwide. Your credit transfers if you move.

How sponsorship works

A contractor sponsors you. That’s the whole model.

You cannot enroll in the apprenticeship on your own. Every apprentice is sponsored by an electrical contractor who employs them full time and pays their instruction fee. If you don’t have a contractor yet, start with Career Prep — that’s what it’s for — or come to an info session and meet the member companies hiring.

Contractors, read this

Sponsorship is how you build the crew you actually want, at a cost you control. Requirements are on the membership page.

  1. 1

    The contractor hires the apprentice

    Sponsorship starts with a job. A member contractor puts the apprentice on payroll as a full-time employee at the company’s own first-year wage — the chapter does not set wages and does not employ anyone.

  2. 2

    The contractor registers them for class

    The sponsoring contractor enrolls the apprentice with the chapter for the coming school year and signs the sponsorship agreement covering attendance, progression and conduct.

  3. 3

    The employer covers tuition

    Instruction fees are billed to the sponsoring contractor, not the apprentice — [$ per year, TBD]. The apprentice is typically responsible only for books, tools and any exam fees.

  4. 4

    Class runs one night a week

    The apprentice works the full week on the contractor’s jobs and attends class one evening — roughly [N] hours of related classroom instruction across a [Sept–May] school year.

  5. 5

    Both sides re-enroll each year

    Progression is annual. The contractor re-enrolls the apprentice for the next year and reports on-the-job hours; the chapter reports grades and attendance back to the contractor.

Curriculum

Four years, in order

The outline below follows the IEC national curriculum. Exact module sequence and hours for the Jacksonville chapter are set by the training committee before the first class.

Year 1

DC and AC theory, electrical safety, hand and power tools, the National Electrical Code as a document — how to find things in it before you have to argue about them.

Year 2

Branch circuits, feeders, conductor sizing and ampacity, raceway and box fill, grounding and bonding, residential and light commercial rough-in.

Year 3

Motors and motor controls, transformers, three-phase systems, commercial service equipment, blueprint reading and load calculations.

Year 4

Advanced code applications, overcurrent protection and coordination, fire alarm and low-voltage interfaces, jobsite supervision, and journeyman exam preparation.

Eligibility

What it takes to apply

  • At least 18 years old
  • High school diploma or equivalent (transcript or GED certificate)
  • Valid driver’s license or state ID
  • Reliable transportation to work and to class
  • Able to read, write and speak English — classes are taught in English
  • Able to perform high-school-level math
  • Physically able to perform the work of the trade
  • Proof of legal residency if not a U.S. citizen

Veterans

Military electrical experience counts. Veterans may test into an advanced year rather than starting at Year 1, and the chapter intends to pursue approval for GI Bill® benefits once the program is registered.

Transfers

Coming from another apprenticeship — another IEC chapter or a different program entirely? Bring your transcript and hours. You’ll be placed by assessment, not by the name on the paperwork.

Career path

  1. Apprentice — years 1–4, paid, in class
  2. Journeyman — licensed, works unsupervised
  3. Master / Contractor — pulls permits, runs the business
